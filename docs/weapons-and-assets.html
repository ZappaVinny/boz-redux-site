Weapons, models, effects and sounds

The parts of a map that go beyond its level: the weapons players use, your own models, the game's effects and projectiles, and sounds. Each has its own tab in the map editor, and all of them are saved in the map project and built into the map's mod. The weapon field reference at the end of the weapons section lists every value a weapon has.

Weapons

The editor's Weapons tab changes the weapons of a map project: their damage, fire rate, ammo, spread and the rest of the game's weapon data. You can also make new weapons from existing ones, bring in weapons from other maps, give a weapon your own model, change its animations and sounds, and add Lua for special behaviour. Changes are saved as you make them and applied when the map builds. Your changes affect your map only; the game's other maps keep their weapons.

The Weapons tab

  • The viewport shows the selected weapon as the player sees it, through the game's eye, with the game's textures. The arms and the gun are one Blender armature with the game's skeletons (the gun under the arms' TAG_WEAPON bone, called "gun position"), and their models move with it the way the game skins them. The first-person view frames the gun exactly as the game does: the game's camera setting (50 unless you changed it) becomes a horizontal angle across a 16:9 screen the way its projection does it (about 71 degrees). Field of view takes the same number as the game's field of view setting: set it to what you play with.

  • Placement (panel):

    • First person / Free view, Arms and Bones switch what the view shows.
    • Position on screen (or click the arms): G moves and R turns the arms and gun together in front of the camera; the arrow puts them back. It is the start of every animation, saved with the weapon ("arms" in its file) and built into all its arm animations.
    • Model: the stock gun or one of your assets (see Your own model).

  • Animation (panel) plays the weapon's animations: pick one, then Play, step a frame or to a change, Loop or drag the Frame slider. Change them in the Animation tab (see Animations); one you changed is marked "(edited)".

  • The list shows the project's weapons. A map made from a whole shipped level lists its guns; an Empty or Debug map starts with the Colt alone. The list is what wall buys and the mystery box offer. Icons: a pencil for a changed weapon, a plus for a new one, an arrow for one from another map.

  • Import from game adds a weapon: one of the map's own, or another map's (Call of the Dead's V-R11, Scavenger and Wunderwaffe DG-2, Ascension's matryoshka dolls and minigun, ...). An imported weapon brings its models, skins, skeleton, animations, animation state machines and projectile. Its sounds, effects and special behaviour belong to its own map and are not brought over: plain guns work, but a wonder weapon (V-R11, Wunderwaffe) crashes the game when it hits a zombie. Give a weapon special behaviour with a Lua script (Add script) instead.

  • New weapon copies the selected weapon under a new name: same model and animations, your values.

  • Properties lists every field, grouped. Each shows its unit; the ? button explains it; the arrow resets a changed value. A * marks a meaning read from the shipped weapons that the game's code has not proven yet.

  • Pack-a-Punch result picks the weapon an upgrade turns it into.

  • Starting weapon (tick box): players start with this weapon instead of the Colt, with its spare rounds (the game's default is 40); they still get the knife and two grenades. Untick it to go back to the Colt. It applies to this map only: other maps keep the Colt.

  • Script (optional) adds a Lua script for special behaviour: see below.

Wall buys pick from the project's list (World tab → the wall buy's Choose…); a mystery box's Weapon pool (its settings → Edit…) picks which listed weapons it rolls.

A weapon with your own model

A weapon can show one of your Weapon assets (made in the Assets tab, see Your own models) instead of its stock gun. It keeps the rest of the gun it is made from: the arms' animations, sounds and effects. Make a New weapon from a stock gun whose animations suit your model (a big two-handed one: the Thundergun), then in its Model panel pick your asset.

  • Its own skeleton: the gun becomes your asset's bones (tag_weapon, tag_clip, tag_flash, ...) under a root at the origin like every stock gun's, so its parts sit where they belong. The stock gun's animations are moved onto them: each of your bones moves the way a stock bone does (the list under Original motion of your bones comes from: tag_clip follows the gun's magazine bone, J_Clip or tag_clip, else the bone its reload moves most; tag_flash its muzzle; everything else the root). Change any of them in the list.
  • Fit model in hand (or click your model) shows a copy of it on the gun position to move (G), turn (R) and scale (S), with the stock gun see-through to line it up against; turn it off to keep the fit. Auto fit lines it up with the stock gun again: its barrel (tag_weapon to tag_flash) along the stock gun's, upright, as long as it and centred on it. Position, Rotation and Scale change the fit by number (in the stock gun's model units).

The build writes your model and its skin on its own skeleton, its textures and materials, the lighter models the game shows while aiming down the sights (on guns that have one) and at a distance (three levels, made with the asset; an asset made before these existed uses its full model for them: make it again), every animation of the gun on its skeleton, and the gun's state machine (a copy of the stock one: same timing, transitions and events) pointing at them.

Animations

The Animation tab changes the selected weapon's animations, one at a time. Its small view is the game's eye, its big view is free to work in, and the rig is ready to pose: click a bone, R turns it, G moves it. Grab gun position to move the gun (the hands come with it: each arm reaches its grip with IK) and hand L / hand R to change a grip.

Arms and gun each have their own timeline. Before a part's first change it plays the original. When you change a pose and then go to another frame, the change is kept and holds from that frame on, until your next change; earlier frames never move. So you can keep the original up to a point and go on frame by frame from there: change, step, adjust, step. The panel shows each part's timeline (for example "original 0-29, yours 30-59, original 60+").

  • The arrow next to Arms or Gun sends that part back to the original from the frame shown (until your next change).
  • Copy pose / Paste pose: copy the arms, the gun or both at this frame, from the Original or from Yours, and paste them at another frame or into another animation; the pasted pose holds from there.
  • Copy original frames here: the original's frames From..To play from the frame shown, then its last pose holds.
  • The arrows step a frame back or forward, or to the previous or next change; Length sets the last frame (30 frames a second).
  • Save animation stores it (it is also stored when you save, switch animations or leave the tab); Reset this animation throws every change away.

Animations are stored in the project (source/weapons/anims/<weapon>/<state>.json: your changes to edit again, and the result at every frame with the IK worked out, which the build uses). The build writes your arms animations into a copy of the weapon's arms state machine and the gun's into its gun state machine, so only that weapon changes. The state machines keep their timing and events (when a reload refills the magazine, for example): an animation much longer or shorter than the stock one still has its events at the stock times.

Weapon scripts

A weapon script runs with self as the weapon: self.name, and the live weapon's methods.

-- weapons/mp40_heavy.lua
shots = 0                       -- saved with the game, like any script global

function on_fire(data)          -- every shot (data.clip: rounds before it)
    shots = shots + 1
    if shots % 10 == 0 then self:set_ammo(nil, self:reserve() + 5) end
end

function on_equip() end         -- taken in hand
function on_given() end         -- bought, rolled from the box, or given by a script
function on_reload() end        -- a reload started

Any script can use boz.weapons (require("boz.weapons")): current(), get(name), give(name), and on a weapon clip(), reserve(), set_ammo(clip, reserve), clip_size(), damage(), set_damage(head, torso, limbs), fire_rate(), set_fire_rate(rpm), in_hand(). These change the weapon for the match; the Weapons tab changes it for good. Game events on the bus: weapon_given, weapon_equipped, weapon_reloaded (data.weapon); weapon_fired reaches boz.on listeners only (every shot).

Weapon project files

The weapon list is source/weapons/index.json; each changed, new or imported weapon has its own source/weapons/<name>.json:

{"name": "mp40_heavy", "base": "mp40", "level": "", "fields": {"m_clipSize": 50,
 "m_damage.m_headDamage": 900, "Upgrade": "Afterburner"}, "script": "weapons/mp40_heavy.lua"}

base is the weapon it is made from (empty: itself), level the map it is imported from (empty: this map's own), display its In-game name (Properties). The game names weapons in its prompts from its own text: a wall buy asks for the string id <weapon>_S_WEAPON (CIngameStatePlaying::UpdatePoiPrompt hashes the weapon's name on with _S_WEAPON). A new weapon has no entry there, so the prompt shows ">>Unknown Hash<<" until you give it an in-game name; boz.prefabs adds that text (boz.text) when the level starts. Empty keeps the game's own name. sounds maps sound fields to the weapon's own sounds ({"reloadSoundSequence": "my_reload"}, the Audio tab's saved sounds): the build gives the field a sound of the weapon's own and points the field at it (Sounds).

How weapons work

Each map has a weapons group (ingame/weapons/weapons_<map>): one entity per weapon with a CPlayerWeapon component, plus the weapon's models, skins, skeleton, animations and two animation state machines (the arms' and the gun's; the arms' live in arms_<map>). The fire mode is a class the game makes from Fire mode (CPlayerWeapon::CreateBehaviour): automatic modes fire at Fire rate; single-shot weapons fire as fast as their fire animation plays, and a reload lasts as long as its animation (the bullet and the end of a reload are events in the animations), so those two come with the animation tools, not this form.

Your own models

The Assets tab brings your own models into the game at the game's level of detail. You import a high-quality model, and the editor makes a game version of it: a few thousand triangles at most and small textures, the way the game's own models are made. You then place the game version in a map, or give a weapon your model (Weapons).

Importing

Import… takes a .blend, .fbx, .glb/.gltf or .obj file. Choose:

  • Kind: Prop (a model to place in maps) or Weapon (a gun: its armature, bones and bone weights are kept, and its parts are found from the bones).
  • Preset: how detailed the game version is.
Preset Weapon Prop
Stock (like the game's own) 1,500 triangles, textures up to 512 800 triangles, textures up to 256
PC (sharper) 4,000 triangles, textures up to 1024 2,500 triangles, textures up to 512

For comparison, the game's guns have about 1,000 to 1,300 triangles and 256 to 512 textures. A busy model (lots of thin spikes, tentacles, teeth) keeps its shape much better on PC; import a gun as a Weapon, since a Prop's budget is about half.

The file is copied into the project (assets/<name>/), and its original location is remembered, so Refresh from source picks up later changes to it (for example a magazine added to the gun). The game version is made straight away.

Making the game version

Make game version runs in the background (the editor stays usable; progress shows in the Log). It:

  1. Loads the model's meshes (from a .blend: all its meshes, with their armatures). The armature with the most bones is the model's; another armature that shares a bone name with it (a magazine with its own tag_clip, as ripped attachments come) is snapped so that bone lines up with the model's, the way the game attaches parts, wherever it sits in the file.

  2. Works out the size: the Source unit (centimetres, metres, inches, millimetres) is guessed from the model's length (a weapon 30 to 200 cm, a prop 10 to 600 cm) unless you set it.

  3. Reduces every mesh to its share of the triangle budget (bigger meshes get more; small parts such as eyes keep at least a few dozen triangles). Each mesh is welded first, so it reduces as one surface without cracks; afterwards every triangle reads its texture coordinates back from the original surface, from the texture island under its centre, so no triangle smears the texture across a seam. Bone weights are kept.

  4. Makes one texture per material: the colour image reduced to a power of two, the biggest surfaces getting the preset's size and smaller ones less (about the same detail per centimetre everywhere). The game has no normal maps, so with Bake normal map on the normal map's detail is shaded into the colour (lit from above and in front). Two-channel normal maps (Call of Duty's, with an empty blue channel) work too. A material without an image gets a small texture of its colour.

  5. Closes what reducing opened, so nothing is see-through: reducing rounds corners off and lets the borders of the model's open shells (parts tucked into each other) creep back, which opens gaps. The reduced surface is pushed out gently to cover the original, and every open border grows back a little so neighbouring shells overlap again. Then the game version is drawn as the game draws it (one side) from six directions against the original: if anything is see-through, it is made again from the original with more triangles (up to five times). Game checks says when it needed more triangles than your budget; a model this busy at the Stock preset may need two or three times as many.

Own budget sets the triangle count yourself; Textures caps the texture size.

The view

The tab has its own scene: the map is never touched. The viewport header switches between High quality, Game and Side by side. The game version is drawn the way the game draws it (the texture only, no lighting). The image editor shows its textures.

The game version is an ordinary mesh: you can touch it up by hand and Save edits keeps your changes (bone weights follow the nearest original vertex). Making it again asks first, because it replaces your edits.

Game checks

Game checks lists the game version's triangles, vertices, textures (and their memory), size and bones, then:

  • Problems (they stop the map from building): more than 65,535 vertices, a model more than 3.2 m from its centre (usually the wrong unit), texture coordinates past -8..8, texture sides that are not powers of two from 4 to 1024, a vertex following more than 4 bones.
  • Warnings: over the triangle budget, textures over the preset, and for weapons a missing part (the magazine, tag_clip: the reload would show none).

Where there is a one-click fix (reduce the budget, halve the textures, use centimetres), a Fix button sits next to it.

Using an asset

Place in map puts a copy at the 3D cursor in the World tab; Add → My assets and the palette's My assets catalog (drag it in) do the same. A placed asset is a model like any other (collision, start state, scripts, prefabs) that refers to the asset: making the asset again updates every copy. An asset placed in the map cannot be removed until its copies are deleted.

The game version is stored in tenths of a centimetre (the game's positions are 16-bit integers, so this keeps small details) and placed at 0.1 scale, the way the game itself places some of its models.

Model project files

assets/<name>/<file>              the source file (a copy)
source/assets/<name>.json         settings: kind, preset, budget, texture size, unit, ...
source/assets/<name>/model.json   the game version: vertices, uvs, triangles, materials;
                                  weapons also bones, bone weights and parts
source/assets/<name>/textures/    one PNG per material
source/assets/<name>/stats.json   the figures and checks shown in the tab

bozkit.assets reads and writes these; blender/boz_suite/asset_build.py makes the game version. Third-party models (ripped game models and the like) belong in your project only: do not publish them.

Effects and projectiles

Scripts can spawn the game's effects and projectiles anywhere, for as long as they want, with boz.effects. A projectile brings the game's own behaviour with it: a Gersch device opens a black hole that pulls zombies in and kills them, a monkey bomb lures them, a bolt explodes where it hits.

The Effects tab

Game effects lists everything the game can spawn. Show filters it:

  • Effects: particles and visual effects (the shared ones, and each map's own).
  • Projectiles: each map's grenades, bolts, lures and devices, with what they do and which weapons fire them (Ascension's bh_bomb projectile is the Gersch device: "Black hole").

Every one works on any map: the build brings whatever a map uses with everything it needs (its models, materials, textures, and the sounds its code plays by name), from whichever map it comes from. Search by name, map or behaviour. Pick one, give it a Name (what scripts call it) and Name it. Scripts can also spawn any of them by the game's name or "0x..." hash without a name: the build finds those in the scripts.

Named effects lists the names: rename or forget them, and Copy script line copies a line of Lua that spawns the selected one where the player aims. A projectile has two switches, and the build gives the map its own copy of it without what they leave out:

  • Hide its model: no model (a black hole without the Gersch device lying on the floor).
  • Stays where spawned: no physics (no gravity or rolling: it stays exactly where a script spawns it; a black hole then opens right where the shot lands).

boz.effects

local effects = require("boz.effects")

-- A one-off effect, removed after two seconds.
effects.spawn("impactRayGun", effects.aim(), {lifetime = 2})

-- A black hole where the player aims, for six seconds, its swirl at chest height.
local hole = effects.projectile("gersch", effects.aim(), {trigger = "open", lifetime = 6, raise = 200})
hole:remove()               -- or close it early (with its own sound)
  • effects.spawn(name, position, options): an effect at {x, y, z} (game centimetres, y up). Options: rotation {x, y, z, w}, lifetime (seconds, then it is removed).
  • effects.projectile(name, position, options): a projectile the game runs. Options: trigger ("open"), lifetime (seconds), raise (centimetres: it floats that far above the position; for one that Stays where spawned), hidden (its own model is not drawn: a black hole without the device lying on the floor), freeze (stay where spawned instead of settling on the floor), lift (centimetres up, default 10, so it does not start in the floor; raise replaces it), rotation. It is the player's (zombies turning to a device ask for its owner, so it always has one). The game sets a new projectile up a frame after it spawns; the trigger, lifetime and hiding are applied then (:ready() says when), so scripts need not wait.
  • effects.aim(): the point the player's crosshair is on (where a shot lands, up to 100 m) and whether it is a live zombie.
  • What they return: :alive(), :ready(), :position(), :move(position), :trigger(name), :hide(), :show(), :freeze(), :lifetime(seconds), :remove().

Names are the Effects tab's names, or the game's own names ("gersch_Effect", "impactRayGun", "iceShatter", ...), or "0x..." hashes; the build bundles every one a script names.

What projectiles do

Behaviour What it does Triggers
Thrown (lifetime) Grenades: explode after their time explode
Bolt Ray gun, launchers: explode where they hit explode
Lure Monkey bomb: zombies are drawn to it
Black hole Gersch device: zombies within 50 m walk into it and die within 1.5 m; a player inside is sent to one of the map's Gersch teleport points (a map without them: nothing happens) open

A black hole's swirl draws about a metre below the device (the device normally lies on the floor and the swirl model is built downwards from it), so a hole spawned on the floor shows its centre under the floor: raise = 200 puts the ball at chest height. A raised black hole kills just as far around it on the floor: its kill reach grows with the height, so zombies walking under it still die.

A black hole normally opens once it has come to rest and lasts 8 seconds; trigger = "open" opens it at once and lifetime sets how long it stays open.

The game kills the zombies a black hole takes without naming who killed them, so they do not count as the player's kills (stats, achievements). A script that wants them counted asks once: require("boz.zombies").credit_kills("SUBJECT_ZOMBIE_GERSCH_DEATH"). The Effects tab's Copy script line adds that line for a black hole.

Example: a gun that opens a black hole where it fires

This example makes idgun, a new weapon copied from the Thundergun (New weapon in the Weapons tab), whose shots open black holes. In the Effects tab, name Ascension's bh_bomb projectile gersch and tick Hide its model and Stays where spawned. In the Weapons tab, set idgun's Fire mode to Single shot (a Thundergun-based weapon would otherwise still fire its own blast; the Thundergun's bullets do no damage, so the black hole does the killing), and give it a script (Add script):

local effects = require("boz.effects")
require("boz.zombies").credit_kills("SUBJECT_ZOMBIE_GERSCH_DEATH")   -- its kills are the player's

-- Every shot: a black hole where the shot lands, for six seconds.
function on_fire(data)
    local where = effects.aim()
    if where then
        effects.projectile("gersch", where, {trigger = "open", lifetime = 6, raise = 200})
    end
end

How effects work

From the game's code (Ghidra names in the game definition): Spawn_ByHash(CSpawnManager, spec hash, position, rotation) creates any entity spec a loaded group holds (Entity_CreateFromSpec, then the CIsTransform's position +0xa8 and rotation +0x98, stored scalar first: s x y z). A projectile is an entity with a CProjectileEntity whose behaviourType (1 thrown, 2 bolt, 4 lure, 6 Gersch device) decides what Projectile_UpdateFlight, Projectile_UpdateGersch and Projectile_Impact do; it keeps its lifetime (ms) at +0xc0, its open state at +0xc7 and its owner at +0x88, and takes events on its subject at +0x28. A Gersch device that receives SUBJECT_SPAWN_GERSCH_EFFECT spawns gersch_Effect (shared), plays gersch_opens and loops gersch_loop (Ascension's sounds), and counts its lifetime down to SUBJECT_TERMINATE (removes the effect, plays gersch_shuts). Zombies (CAIControllerZombie) ask ProjectileManager_FindNearest for an open device within 5000 units, walk into it, and die (SUBJECT_ZOMBIE_GERSCH_DEATH) within m_proximityRadius x 100 (the projectile's +0x90, metres, measured in 3D from its centre: raise widens it to sqrt(r² + raise²)). The swirl (gersch_Effect, a billboard model) spawns at the device's position and rotation and draws from 22 to 205 units below it. WeaponManager_UpdateAim ray-casts the crosshair every frame into the weapon manager's +0xcc.

The catalog is bozkit/effects.py, the bundling bozkit/bundle.py (Asset formats); the names are source/effects.json.

Sounds

The Audio tab replaces the game's sounds with your own. It has two parts:

  • Game sounds: every sound your map plays, as slots you can hear and replace.
  • Your sounds: the processor every sound goes through before it can be used: Import a recording, shape it, Preview it as the game will play it, then Save.

Only saved sounds go in slots. Changing a saved sound's settings asks you to Save again; the slots using it play the new version from the next build.

Game sounds

Pick Show the sounds the first time (it needs the asset library). The list shows each sound with its length; Show filters it:

Interface Menu and interface sounds

Type in the list's search field to find a sound by name or by what plays it ("Reload", "ak74u").

Slots are named after what plays them: a weapon's sound is "Thundergun: Fire"; sounds the game names are named after it ("Carpenter pickup", "Dog spawn"); voice lines start with the character ("Takeo: …"); a sound many weapons share names the first and how many more ("colt45 +10: Empty click"); the rest show where they live and their number ("Shared 0x…"). Played by lists everything that plays the selected sound.

The game stores weapon sounds in pieces, and the names say which kind:

  • Parts ("ak74u: Reload part 2 of 4"): a reload is several short recordings (mag out, mag in, bolt, ...) played in order with set waits between them (the ak74u's: 350, 350, 550 and 250 ms), timed to the animation. Replace the part you want to change.
  • Takes ("ak74u: Fire take 1 of 2"): a shot plays one of a few recordings picked at random, so firing does not sound the same every time. Replace every take for a new fire sound (the same sound in each is fine).

The wave view on the right draws the selected slot's original (grey, top) over your replacement (orange, bottom); for one of your sounds, the recording (with what the trim cuts shaded) over the game version. Both share one time scale, with a tick every 0.1 s.

  • Play original plays the game's sound (as the game stores it).
  • Replace with puts one of your saved sounds in the slot; Play then plays yours.
  • Reset gives the slot its original back.

A slot is one recording of the game. Everything that plays it plays your sound: a weapon made from another (New weapon) plays its base's sounds, so replacing one changes both.

A weapon's own sounds

The Weapons tab's Sounds panel gives the selected weapon sounds of its own: for Fire, Last round, Empty click, Reload, Reload (rounds left), Equip / pickup and Bought (player), Use one sound picks one of your saved sounds. The build gives sequence fields a new sequence and direct-play fields a new sound spec (with the volume and pitch of the sound it replaces), so:

  • one recording can replace a reload the game plays in four parts, with nothing left over;
  • the weapon it is made from keeps its own sounds (a new reload sound on idgun, from the black hole example, leaves the Thundergun's alone).

Equip / pickup plays when the weapon is equipped, including after a wall buy, but also on ordinary weapon switches. Bought (player) is the buyer's sound on a wall purchase. The shared wall-buy click plays separately and is not replaced by either field. These native fields do not cover every way a weapon can be acquired. For a grant-only sound, assign a saved sound to Bought (player), then call its generated spec from the weapon script's on_given() callback:

function on_given()
    require("boz.audio").play(self.name .. "_playerBuySound")
end

That callback runs for CWeaponManager::GiveWeapon (wall buy, mystery box, and boz.weapons.give). A wall purchase may also play the native Bought (player) field, so this example can play the sound twice on that path; use it only when that trade-off is acceptable.

Your recording carries its own timing: line it up with the weapon's reload animation (the wave view shows its length). Use the game's (✕) goes back. Below, the panel lists the game sounds the weapon still plays, which you can replace as slots (for every weapon that plays them).

Your sounds

Import… reads any file Blender's audio library reads (WAV, MP3, OGG, FLAC, AIFF, ...). The recording is kept in the project as it is; nothing is changed until you Save.

Setting What it does
Rate 22050 Hz (better quality; most weapon sounds) or 11025 Hz (half the size; most voices and effects). The game plays only these two.
Trim start / end Seconds cut from the start and the end
Normalize Brings the loudest point to -1 dB first
Gain Then raises or lowers the level (dB)
Fade in / out Seconds of fade at the start and the end

Stereo recordings are mixed down to mono (the game's sounds are mono). Recording plays the imported file; Preview (game) plays it with the settings, through the game's own compression and back, so you hear exactly what players will. Save makes the game version.

Sounds in the game

Build & Run puts your sounds in. A sound of the map's own groups is replaced in the level the build makes; a shared or character sound is replaced in a copy of the game's group the mod carries (the client serves a mod's files in place of the game's), so it changes wherever that mod is enabled. The sound's volume, pitch, polyphony and everything that plays it stay the game's.

Not yet: the streamed music (mp3 files) and placing new sound emitters.

Sound project files

source/sounds/<name>.json        the sound's settings and where it was imported from
source/sounds/<name>/source.wav  the imported recording (as read)
source/sounds/<name>/game.wav    the saved game version (mono, 16-bit, the chosen rate)
source/sounds/slots.json         {"<group path>#0x<sound hash>": "<name>"}: the filled slots

The formats and the slot catalog are in bozkit/sound.py and Asset formats.
